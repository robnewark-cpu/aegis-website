## Executive summary

Texas litigation is high volume in some practices and high complexity in others. Personal-injury dockets in major metros, commercial cases in Dallas and Houston, energy disputes, employment matters, insurance coverage fights, and docket-heavy defense work all share a structural problem: the work is repetitive enough to automate badly. A badly automated firm sends the same discovery checklist into a case with a protective-order overlay it does not fit, calendars a deadline from a template instead of from the order, and then blames “the system” when a setting is missed.

This article explains litigation workflow automation as an operations discipline for Texas firms. It describes stages that almost every dispute practice recognizes—intake and conflicts, engagement, pleadings and service tracking as the *firm* understands them, discovery, expert and vendor coordination, pretrial, resolution, and file closing—without reciting Texas Rule of Civil Procedure numbers, local-rule numbers, or case law. Those authorities change and are court-specific. Readers must verify the current Texas Disciplinary Rules of Professional Conduct, the current statewide procedural rules as applicable, and the local rules and electronic-filing administrative procedures of the court where the matter is pending.

Automation belongs in the firm’s system of record: tasks with owners, documents generated from matter data, billing that follows activity, and permissions that keep a mass-tort file from being visible to everyone in a four-hundred-lawyer office. Court electronic filing in Texas is widely used; this article will not invent the steps of any e-filing portal. The practice platform organizes the work. The court remains authoritative for the docket.

## Key definitions

**Workflow** is a defined sequence of tasks with a triggering event, an owner role, due-date logic the *firm* chooses, completion criteria, and an audit trail. It is not a sticky note, and it is not the court’s docket.

**Automation** means the system creates tasks, applies templates, or routes approvals without someone retyping the same list. It does not mean the system practices law.

**Docketing** in firm language is the recording of settings, deadlines, and obligations. **Official docketing** is what the court records. Confusing them is a classic failure.

**Playbook** is a practice group’s written way of handling a matter type (for example, first-party insurance defense versus commercial contract). Playbooks should cite current rules by reference, not by frozen, possibly outdated numbers copied into a wiki.

**Discovery workflow** is the firm’s process for identifying, preserving, collecting, reviewing, and producing information, plus tracking incoming discovery from other parties. This article does not invent Texas discovery-rule text.

**Work breakdown** is how a case is split among partners, associates, paralegals, and vendors (court reporters, experts, e-discovery providers). Automation assigns and tracks; it does not replace supervision.

**Gate** is a required human approval (file a pleading, produce documents, disburse trust funds, send a client a settlement statement).

**Texas Disciplinary Rules of Professional Conduct** are the professional-conduct rules Texas lawyers must actually read in their current form. This article discusses concepts—competence, confidentiality, communication, supervision—not numbered substitutes.

## Industry terminology

**Plaintiff high-volume vs. defense panel vs. commercial boutique.** Volume practices need ruthless checklist discipline and conflict hygiene. Defense panel practices need reporting clocks that match carrier guidelines (contractual, not statutory as used here). Commercial boutiques need flexible tasks and stronger related-party conflicts.

**e-Filing.** Many Texas courts use electronic filing. The statewide landscape has been built around electronic submission for a long time, but **each court and clerk may have administrative procedures you must follow**. This article will not invent portal clicks, accepted file types, or service mechanics.

**Service and citation.** How a defendant is served is a legal and procedural question. Workflow can track “service packet prepared” and “affidavit received.” It cannot decide sufficiency.

**Docket control and scheduling orders.** Many Texas cases will have court-issued schedules. Firm workflows should *attach* those dates to the matter, not overwrite them with a generic 90-day template.

**MDL and multi-county.** Some Texas litigation spans many venues. The stack must allow multiple settings without mixing style captions.

**IOLTA and settlements.** Settlement funds, if held, belong in trust accounting with three-way reconciliation. Workflow can require a disbursement checklist. Aegis does not hold the money.

**Federal vs. state.** A Texas firm may file in state court one day and in a U.S. District Court the next. PACER and CM/ECF are federal systems. Do not reuse a state playbook blindly. See the federal companion article.

**AI drafting.** Generative tools are not Texas workflow. If used, they need governance (companion AI article). Do not auto-file model output.

## What is Texas litigation workflow automation?

It is the decision to treat a lawsuit as a managed project with legal judgment at defined gates.

Without automation, Texas litigation still happens: talented people email each other. With automation, the firm can answer: what is open, who owns it, what is blocked on the client, what discovery requests are outstanding *as the firm tracked them*, what invoices are unbilled, and who accessed the file.

A mature litigation workflow typically includes:

- **Intake and conflicts** before the first public filing.
- **Engagement and fee setup**, including trust retainers if used.
- **Matter workspace**: parties, counsel, insurers, claims numbers, venue (as data, not as a jurisdiction opinion).
- **Pleading and motion tasking** driven by events the firm records (complaint received, answer due *as calendared from the order or rule the lawyer applied*).
- **Discovery** as phases with document holds internal to the firm, vendor folders, and production logs.
- **Experts and vendors** as related records, not random emails.
- **Pretrial** checklists that start from the actual scheduling order.
- **Resolution** including settlement statements and trust disbursement gates.
- **Closeout**, conflicts preservation, and retention.

## How does workflow automation work in a litigation practice?

**Event in, tasks out.** A matter status change (“discovery opened”) creates a set of tasks assigned by role. Escalations fire when a task is overdue. Notifications go to owners. That pattern is how enterprise workflow engines work generally; AegisOS describes workflows in those terms.

**Data in, documents out.** Document automation fills captions and party names from the matter record. The lawyer still verifies the style against the court’s current requirements. E-signature may be appropriate for engagement letters or certain acknowledgments; it is not automatically valid for every litigation instrument. Check the transaction.

**People in, permissions out.** Role-based access control limits who sees a sensitive file (employment, minors’ information as the firm classifies it, high-profile commercial disputes). Multi-tenant isolation keeps other law firms on the same platform out of the file. Audit logging records access.

**Time in, invoices out.** Litigation bleeds unbilled time. Tasks that require a time entry, or billing reports that show activity without time, are how finance and supervising partners catch leakage. AegisPay and LexFlow billing features apply as publicly described.

**Client in, communication tracked.** Portals and secure messaging reduce the “settlement authority discussed only in a text message” problem. Communication as a professional concept still requires lawyers to tell clients about significant developments; a portal status bar is not a complete conversation.

**Money in, fiduciary gates.** If a Texas firm holds settlement proceeds, workflow should require dual control according to the firm’s own policy and current disciplinary rules the firm has actually read. Three-way reconciliation remains a finance calendar, not a task you skip because the case settled “cleanly.”

**Court systems to the side.** Staff download a setting from the court’s system and enter or attach it to the matter. The workflow does not scrape a court website unless the firm has a separately diligence-approved integration—which this article does not claim for Aegis.

## Why does automation matter for Texas dispute firms?

**Volume.** Some Texas practices open more files in a month than a commercial boutique opens in a year. Human memory does not scale.

**Staffing leverage.** Paralegals and docketing clerks are force multipliers only if the checklist is the same on Tuesday as on Thursday.

**Supervision.** Texas firms, like others, remain responsible for work done by juniors and nonlawyers under professional-conduct concepts of supervision. Workflow makes incomplete work visible.

**Carrier and client reporting.** Defense firms miss reporting clocks not because they cannot write, but because the clock lived in someone’s inbox.

**Multi-venue reality.** A Houston file and a Panhandle file should not share a caption template by accident. Matter data should drive the style.

**Lateral movement.** Texas markets hire aggressively. A matter-centric system is how files survive a lawyer’s departure.

**Confidentiality at scale.** Large Texas offices have more people who might peek. RBAC and logs are how confidentiality stays operational.

**Settlement money.** High-value personal-injury and commercial settlements increase trust-accounting risk. Automation of checklists is helpful; Aegis still does not hold funds.

## When should a firm automate a stage—or refuse to?

**Automate when** the step is repeatable, the completion test is clear, and a miss is costly (conflict check, open a file, request a medical record *as a task*, calendar from a signed scheduling order, close a file).

**Refuse to automate when** the step is a legal conclusion (limitations, venue, whether service was valid, whether a privilege applies). The workflow may create a task named “lawyer determines X.” It should not populate X from a hidden formula marketed as “AI statute tracker” unless the firm is prepared to own that product as if it were a junior associate who never went to law school.

**Refuse to auto-file.** Human gate before anything is submitted through a court’s electronic system.

**Automate later** for exotic matter types. Do not delay conflict and trust controls while waiting for a perfect commercial-litigation playbook.

**Escalate when** a case has a court-specific standing order the playbook does not know. Local rules must be read. This article will not invent them.

**Time the rollout** away from a docket call week for the pilot group.

## What are the benefits of litigation workflow automation?

- Fewer silent drops between intake and first filing.
- Conflict checks that actually occur.
- Discovery tasks that survive vacation.
- Captions that match matter data more often.
- Time captured nearer to hearings.
- Supervisors who can see stalled files.
- Clients who see status without leaking files into consumer apps.
- Settlement disbursement checklists that include trust verification.
- Onboarding that is not folklore.

These benefits appear when the playbook is maintained. A stale playbook automates yesterday’s mistakes.

## What are the risks of litigation workflow automation?

- **Template worship.** Staff follow the checklist instead of the order.
- **Deadline fiction.** Auto-calculated dates that were never compared to the rule or order the lawyer must apply.
- **Over-notification.** Everyone ignores the noise.
- **Under-permissioning of covering lawyers** so that automation assigns a task the assignee cannot see.
- **E-discovery vendors outside the permissions model.**
- **False comfort.** A green workflow bar is not a continuance.
- **Local-rule blindness.** Statewide playbooks fail in courts with extra administrative procedures.
- **AI-generated discovery responses** pasted into production without review.
- **Payment-rail confusion** if clients pay online toward the wrong matter. Confirm AegisPay behavior in a demo; no ACH-origination claim.

## Step-by-step workflow: from intake to closeout

This is a firm operating procedure example, not a Texas Rule of Civil Procedure.

**Step 1 — Intake record.** Names, aliases, employers, insurers, related companies, opposing counsel if known, venue if known as a fact the client stated. Do not skip related parties because the caller is “a friend from law school.”

**Step 2 — Automated conflict check.** Review hits. Document the lawyer’s decision. LexFlow is publicly described as including automated conflict checking.

**Step 3 — Engagement.** Generate documents from matter fields; e-sign if appropriate. Open billing and, if funds will be held, a trust sub-ledger. Confirm payment methods in an AegisPay demo if online payments are part of the plan.

**Step 4 — Matter workspace.** Parties, claims numbers, responsible attorney, team roles. Apply RBAC. Turn on portal access only for people who should have it.

**Step 5 — Playbook selection.** Choose the matter-type workflow. Immediately attach any court scheduling order as the date source of truth for settings the court controls.

**Step 6 — Early-case tasks.** Whatever the playbook lists—preserve internal evidence, request client documents, notify carriers—as *tasks*, not as buried emails.

**Step 7 — Pleading cycle.** Document automation for captions; lawyer drafts substance; human gate; designated e-filer follows **that court’s** current administrative procedures. Store the file-stamped copy on the matter.

**Step 8 — Discovery cycle.** Tasks for outgoing and incoming sets, vendor coordination, privilege logging as the firm performs it, production numbering. Do not let a generative tool be the privilege log.

**Step 9 — Pretrial.** Rebuild the checklist from the live order. Deposition scheduling as tasks with locations and reporters as vendor records.

**Step 10 — ADR or trial prep.** Same principle: tasks and documents on the matter; covering lawyers can see what they are allowed to see.

**Step 11 — Resolution.** Settlement statement checklist; trust three-way before disbursement; client communication of what they are signing. IOLTA remains a general construct—follow current Texas trust-account authorities, which this article does not number.

**Step 12 — Closeout.** Final bill, final trust zeroing or remaining balance handled per current rules the firm verifies, conflict retention, disable portal, archive per firm policy.

**Step 13 — Retro.** Which tasks were skipped? Update the playbook. If a local administrative procedure changed, change the playbook that week.

## Risks

Automation can industrialize error. A wrong venue field in the matter record can flow into twenty documents. A playbook that assumes electronic service mechanics that a particular court does not use will train staff to be wrong efficiently. Multi-county firms may need playbook variants—without inventing local rules in this article, the operational point is: variants exist, so own them.

Staff may hide work in personal tools to escape a noisy workflow engine. That recreates the problem. Tune notifications.

Do not let workflow replace reading the Texas Disciplinary Rules of Professional Conduct or the order in the file.

## Best practices

1. One owner per playbook (a partner, not “the IT person”).
2. Dates from orders beat dates from folklore.
3. Human gates before filing and before trust disbursement.
4. Matter-level permissions in large Texas offices.
5. Store file-stamped copies on the matter the same day.
6. Tie time entry to the tasks that created the work.
7. Keep e-filing credentials individual.
8. Review playbooks when courts issue standing-order changes.
9. Train covering lawyers how to find the live checklist.
10. Verify current professional-conduct rules on a cadence.
11. Keep federal and state playbooks distinct.
12. Confirm payment rails; never claim Aegis ACH or fund custody.

## Common mistakes

- Calendaring from a blog post about Texas procedure instead of from the order and current official rules.
- Using one “Texas litigation” template for Travis County and a rural district without checking administrative differences.
- Letting a chatbot draft special exceptions or discovery and filing the draft.
- Shared e-filing logins.
- No conflict check on additional defendants added six months later.
- Settlement money in operating because “it’s almost ours.”
- Portal disabled, so medical records live in SMS.
- Measuring automation success by number of tasks created rather than by missed-setting rate.
- Copying an Oklahoma playbook (or any other state’s) and changing the header to Texas.
- Treating Edmond-based Aegis as a Texas bar resource. It is a software vendor publishing education.

## FAQ

### What should a Texas litigation playbook contain?

Matter-type stages, role owners, document templates, and explicit reminders to read the current order and current official rules. It should not freeze possibly outdated rule numbers as if they were software constants. Include gates for filing and for money.

### How does workflow relate to Texas e-filing?

Workflow can remind a human to file and can store the return. The human follows the court’s administrative procedures. This article does not document portal steps. Do not assume Aegis submits filings.

### Why do discovery workflows fail first?

Because discovery is both repetitive and strategic. Checklists help production hygiene. They cannot decide objections. Firms also lose incoming-request tracking in email. Put requests on the matter as records.

### When should a defense firm automate carrier reporting?

When the guideline clocks are clear and the matter data includes the claim identifiers the carrier expects. Keep the report content lawyer-reviewed. Carrier portals are additional systems in the stack.

### What professional-conduct issues appear in automation?

Confidentiality (who sees the workflow), supervision (whether tasks are actually reviewed), communication (clients not learning significant events from a portal tile alone), and competence (lawyers who let templates substitute for reading). Verify current Texas Disciplinary Rules of Professional Conduct.

### How should multi-plaintiff or multi-defendant files be structured?

Usually one matter per court case, with parties as related records—or a parent matter with children if the firm truly bills and staffs that way. Pick a model and do not mix. Conflicts must search all names.

### Why is three-way reconciliation part of a litigation article?

Because Texas litigation often holds client or settlement funds. A completed workflow that skips fiduciary accounting is an incomplete closeout. LexFlow is described as supporting trust/IOLTA accounting with three-way reconciliation. Aegis is not the bank.

### When is document automation inappropriate?

When the instrument’s validity depends on formalities the template does not capture, or when captions must match a court-specific standing requirement the matter data lacks. Always review. E-signature is not universal for all litigation documents.

### How do federal cases in Texas fit this workflow?

Use a distinct playbook. PACER and CM/ECF are the federal electronic systems. Follow that district’s administrative procedures. Do not invent NextGen dates. See the federal companion article.

### What should firms ask in a LexFlow demo for Texas litigation?

Ask to see workflow configuration, matter party records, conflict search, document automation, billing on a litigation file, portal permissions, audit log access, and trust disbursement recording. Ask what is *not* included (court filing, ACH origination, fund custody, FedRAMP).
